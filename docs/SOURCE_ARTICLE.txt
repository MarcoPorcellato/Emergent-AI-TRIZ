Do Large Language Models Rediscover TRIZ?
The Latent TRIZ Hypothesis and a Public Experiment on the Internal Geometry of Inventive Thinking
By Marco Porcellato
Abstract
What if the 40 Inventive Principles of TRIZ are not only a human-designed methodology, but a partial description of a more general algebra of inventive thinking?

Genrikh Altshuller and his collaborators examined a vast body of inventions, removed their domain-specific details, and identified recurring transformations that resolved contradictions across otherwise unrelated technical systems. Modern large language models undergo a different but intriguingly parallel process: they compress enormous quantities of human-generated text, including descriptions of problems, solutions, inventions, failures, discoveries, and strategies.

I propose the Latent TRIZ Hypothesis:

When a sequence model is exposed to enough examples of human problem solving, at least some recurring contradiction-resolution strategies become encoded as domain-general, reusable, and causally active internal representations—even when those strategies are not explicitly named as TRIZ principles.

This article introduces the hypothesis and proposes Project Latent TRIZ, an open experiment combining TRIZ, mechanistic interpretability, sparse autoencoders, the Jacobian Lens, and activation steering.

The goal is not merely to ask whether an LLM can explain or apply TRIZ after being prompted. The deeper question is whether models have already discovered internal equivalents of inventive principles—and whether we can locate, manipulate, and test them causally.

While studying how language models represent knowledge internally, I began to notice a powerful analogy between the history of TRIZ and the emergence of abstract representations inside neural networks.

That analogy led to a testable scientific hypothesis.


From Patents to Principles
TRIZ did not begin with a list of 40 clever suggestions.

Altshuller and his collaborators began with inventions. They investigated how real technical contradictions had been resolved, discarded routine solutions, abstracted away the specific industry and physical implementation, and searched for recurring transformations.

Historical descriptions vary in how they count the full patent corpus, but the official Altshuller Foundation states that more than 40,000 inventions were analyzed to identify 40 recurring techniques. AITRIZ describes a larger screening process in which approximately 200,000 patent disclosures were considered and roughly 40,000 breakthrough inventions were generalized into typical modifications and, ultimately, the 40 Inventive Principles. citeturn175544search1turn175544search12

The essential process was therefore:

concrete inventions → recurring contradiction patterns → abstraction → reusable inventive principles

A principle such as Segmentation removes almost all the original details of an invention. It no longer matters whether the initial problem involved a tire, a manufacturing line, a software architecture, or an organization.

What remains is a transformation:

[ \text{one integrated system} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{multiple coordinated parts} ]

The same can be said of other principles:

[ \operatorname{Invert}(x) ]

[ \operatorname{Dynamize}(x) ]

[ \operatorname{Separate}(x) ]

[ \operatorname{IntroduceFeedback}(x) ]

[ \operatorname{AddIntermediary}(x,y) ]

Seen this way, the principles are not merely prompts or suggestions. They resemble operators that transform one representation of a problem into another.

TRIZ made these latent regularities explicit in symbolic, human-readable form.

The central question of this project is whether LLM training may make some of the same regularities explicit in geometric form.


The Parallel with Large Language Models
A language model is trained to predict the next token. But predicting text across billions or trillions of examples requires more than memorizing isolated phrases.

To predict well, a model benefits from representing recurring latent variables behind the text:

objects and their properties;
agents and intentions;
causal relations;
temporal sequences;
contradictions and trade-offs;
transformations that turn one state into another;
strategies that repeatedly lead from problems to solutions.

The training process can be viewed as a form of extreme compression:

human-generated text → prediction pressure → internal representations → reusable computational patterns

This is not identical to Altshuller’s research process. Altshuller and his colleagues consciously selected, interpreted, classified, and formalized inventions. Gradient descent does none of that explicitly.

Yet both processes face a similar compression problem:

How can a very large number of concrete examples be represented through a much smaller number of reusable regularities?

In TRIZ, the result is visible to us as principles, contradiction patterns, standards, and laws of technical system evolution.

In an LLM, the result may be distributed across activation directions, sparse features, attention circuits, MLP computations, and intermediate representational spaces.

This leads to the possibility that the 40 Inventive Principles are not arbitrary categories imposed on invention, but a human-readable approximation of deeper recurring transformations that any sufficiently capable learning system might find useful.


The Latent TRIZ Hypothesis
I propose two versions of the hypothesis.
The Weak Latent TRIZ Hypothesis
Pretrained language models contain internal representations that correspond to at least some TRIZ inventive principles, and those representations generalize across domains.

Under this version, examples of Segmentation in mechanical engineering, software, biology, logistics, and marketing would activate a shared internal structure even when their vocabulary and physical content differ.

This would already be significant. It would mean that the model has not stored every case as an unrelated linguistic pattern. It has organized some of them around a common abstract transformation.

However, it would not demonstrate independent rediscovery. A pretrained model may have encountered TRIZ books, websites, training materials, patent classifications, or explanations of the principles.
The Strong Latent TRIZ Hypothesis
A neural sequence model can develop internal representations equivalent to TRIZ inventive principles by learning from unlabeled problem–solution examples, without receiving TRIZ terminology, definitions, principle labels, or canonical TRIZ examples.

This is the stronger and more scientifically interesting claim.

It proposes that recurring inventive operators may emerge because they are useful structures for modeling problem solving—not merely because the model has memorized descriptions of TRIZ.

The strong version requires a controlled experiment.


Why This Hypothesis Is Now Testable
Until recently, we could mainly judge language models from their outputs.

We could ask whether a model solved a problem, but not easily determine which internal representation produced its answer.

That is changing.

In the controlled Othello-GPT experiment, a Transformer trained only to predict legal game moves developed an internal representation of the board state despite receiving no explicit board representation or game rules. More importantly, interventions on that internal representation changed the model’s predictions in ways consistent with a counterfactual board state. citeturn654930academia26

Representation Engineering and Contrastive Activation Addition have shown that high-level behavioral properties can sometimes be identified in activation space and manipulated by adding or subtracting steering vectors during inference. citeturn442735academia48turn442735academia50

More recently, Anthropic introduced the Jacobian Lens and the concept of J-space. Their research suggests that language models maintain a relatively small set of verbalizable internal representations that can be reported, manipulated, and used in flexible internal reasoning. Directly replacing or injecting some of those representations can redirect the model’s subsequent behavior. Anthropic is careful not to claim that this proves consciousness, and the J-lens is explicitly presented as an incomplete approximation of the model’s internal workspace. Nevertheless, it provides a new way to inspect causally relevant concepts before they become output tokens. citeturn909087search0turn909087search2

These methods allow us to move beyond:

“The model generated a TRIZ-like solution.”

toward the much stronger question:

“Did the model represent a domain-independent inventive operator internally, and did that representation causally contribute to the solution?”


What Is New About This Experiment
Research projects such as AutoTRIZ, TRIZ-GPT, TRIZ Agents, and TRIZ-RAGNER are already investigating valuable combinations of TRIZ and language models. They use LLMs to automate TRIZ workflows, recognize contradictions, retrieve structured TRIZ knowledge, or coordinate specialized agents. citeturn265191academia44turn265191academia47turn265191academia46turn265191academia45

Those approaches primarily ask:

Can an LLM apply TRIZ when TRIZ knowledge, instructions, retrieval systems, or workflows are supplied to it?

Project Latent TRIZ asks a different question:

Has the model already learned internal abstractions equivalent to parts of TRIZ before we explicitly tell it to use TRIZ?

This reverses the usual relationship.

TRIZ is no longer only a methodology supplied to the model.

It becomes a scientific probe for investigating what the model has independently abstracted from human problem solving.


Project Latent TRIZ
I propose developing the experiment publicly, with open hypotheses, datasets, evaluation protocols, code, intermediate findings, and negative results.

The initial project would proceed through six stages.
Stage 1: Build a Cross-Domain Inventive Principle Dataset
Starting with all 40 principles would create unnecessary ambiguity. Many principles overlap, some are highly physical, and a single invention may legitimately instantiate several principles.

The first dataset should therefore focus on a smaller set of relatively distinguishable transformations, potentially including:

Segmentation
Taking Out
Local Quality
Inversion
Dynamics
Another Dimension
Feedback
Intermediary

For each principle, the dataset should contain problem–solution pairs from multiple domains:

mechanical engineering;
software architecture;
biology;
logistics;
organizational design;
marketing and communication;
product design;
everyday problem solving.

The dataset must not simply repeat the language used in TRIZ manuals.

For example, Segmentation examples should not all contain words such as divide, split, modular, or parts. Otherwise, a probe might classify vocabulary rather than an abstract transformation.

Each case should include:

a contradiction or constrained problem;
an initial system state;
a proposed transformation;
a resulting state;
one or more expert TRIZ labels;
lexical and semantic control cases;
near-miss solutions;
solutions using different principles to resolve similar contradictions.

Since TRIZ principles are not necessarily mutually exclusive, this should be treated as a multi-label representation problem, not a simplistic 40-class classification task.


Stage 2: Separate Recognition from Independent Emergence
The experiment should contain two complementary tracks.
Track A: Pretrained Open Models
This track asks whether existing models already contain latent TRIZ-like structures.

The first testbed can be Gemma 3 270M. It is small enough to inspect layer by layer on consumer hardware, while Google reports that it was trained on six trillion tokens and provides both base and instruction-tuned variants. Google’s Gemma Scope 2 ecosystem also includes sparse autoencoders and transcoders trained across Gemma 3 layers. citeturn195925search0turn438245search1

Gemma 270M is a microscope, not the final judge.

Its small size makes experimentation fast, but a failure to find sophisticated cross-domain inventive abstractions would not disprove the hypothesis. The model may simply lack sufficient representational or reasoning capacity.

The experiment should subsequently scale to:

Gemma 3 1B and 4B;
base and instruction-tuned variants;
one or more Qwen models;
eventually larger open models where computationally feasible.
Track B: Controlled Emergence
This track is necessary to test the strong hypothesis.

A small Transformer would be trained from scratch on a controlled corpus of problem–solution sequences in which:

no TRIZ terminology appears;
no principle names appear;
canonical TRIZ examples are excluded;
the same underlying transformations recur across unrelated domains;
some transformations are held out from certain domains;
new test cases are generated after the training set is frozen.

This would be analogous in spirit to Othello-GPT.

The model would see only sequences describing problems, constraints, transformations, and outcomes. If a domain-independent representation of Segmentation or Inversion emerged anyway, direct memorization of TRIZ documentation would no longer be a sufficient explanation.

The controlled model need not initially be a powerful conversational LLM. A much smaller, microGPT-like Transformer may be scientifically cleaner because its training data and complete developmental history would be known.

Track A tests whether the structures exist in real pretrained models.

Track B tests whether they can emerge independently from recurring patterns in problem solving.


Stage 3: Map the Internal Representations
For every problem and candidate solution, the experiment would record the model’s residual-stream activations across layers.

The first analyses would include:

linear and nonlinear probes;
contrastive activation differences;
leave-one-domain-out classification;
representational similarity analysis;
PCA for visualization, while remembering that a two-dimensional projection is not the latent space itself;
sparse autoencoder features;
Gemma Scope feature analysis;
logit-lens and Jacobian-lens readouts;
activation patching and causal tracing.

A simple test would be:

Can a probe trained only on mechanical, biological, and software examples identify Segmentation in previously unseen marketing or organizational examples?

If it cannot transfer across domains, it may be detecting subject matter rather than an inventive principle.

If it transfers reliably, we have evidence for a domain-general representation—but still only correlational evidence.

That is why steering is decisive.


Stage 4: Test the Representations Causally
Suppose we identify a candidate latent direction associated with Segmentation:

[ v_{\text{Segmentation}} ]

At a selected layer, the normal model activation is:

[ h ]

We intervene by replacing it with:

[ h' = h + \alpha v_{\text{Segmentation}} ]

where (\alpha) controls the steering strength.

The model would then solve a new contradiction without being told to use TRIZ or Segmentation.

We compare:

negative steering;
no steering;
moderate positive steering;
strong positive steering;
norm-matched random vectors;
unrelated principle vectors;
lexical and stylistic control vectors.

The central question is not whether the word segmentation becomes more likely.

It is whether the model becomes more likely to propose solutions that expert evaluators independently recognize as implementing Segmentation, even when the output never uses words such as divide, split, or modular.

The inverse intervention is equally important:

[ h' = h - \alpha v_{\text{Segmentation}} ]

or an explicit projection removing that component.

If positive steering increases Segmentation-based solutions and ablation reduces them, while unrelated capabilities remain substantially intact, the representation is not merely correlated with the principle. It participates causally in producing that class of solution.


Stage 5: Test Composition
TRIZ principles are often used in combination.

This creates a more ambitious test.

Suppose we identify two candidate representations:

[ v_{\text{Segmentation}} ]

and

[ v_{\text{Dynamics}} ]

We can then test:

[ h' = h + \alpha v_{\text{Segmentation}} + \beta v_{\text{Dynamics}} ]

Does the model produce solutions that are simultaneously modular and dynamically adaptable?

If the effects compose predictably, the representations begin to resemble an algebra of transformations, rather than isolated behavioral associations.

The model may also reveal that the principles are not orthogonal axes.

They may instead behave as:

overlapping directions;
sparse combinations of features;
families of related vectors;
nonlinear manifolds;
context-dependent circuits;
cones in an overcomplete representational space.

This would not invalidate the hypothesis. It might tell us that the human list of 40 principles is a useful symbolic compression of a richer underlying geometry.


Stage 6: Replicate Across Models and Evaluators
Any strong conclusion requires replication.

The same principle should ideally be detectable:

across unrelated problem domains;
in base and instruction-tuned models;
across multiple model sizes;
in different model families;
under different prompt formulations;
with independently written evaluation cases;
by multiple TRIZ experts working blind to the intervention condition.

The project should preregister its primary metrics before the final evaluation set is opened.

Human judges should assess:

whether the proposed solution resolves the contradiction;
which principle or combination of principles it uses;
feasibility;
novelty;
constraint adherence;
whether it merely repeats TRIZ terminology without performing the transformation.

Public science must also publish failures. If a principle does not produce a stable representation, that result is as informative as a successful one.


What Would Count as Strong Evidence?
A visually attractive PCA cluster would not be enough.

A linear probe with high accuracy would not be enough.

A model explaining the definition of Segmentation would certainly not be enough.

Strong evidence would require several findings together.
1. Cross-domain decodability
A representation learned from some domains predicts the same principle in unseen domains.
2. Lexical independence
The effect survives after removing principle names, canonical phrases, obvious synonyms, and repeated surface structures.
3. Causal specificity
Adding the candidate representation selectively increases solutions using the target principle—not merely creativity, verbosity, positivity, or compliance.
4. Bidirectional control
Positive steering increases use of the principle, while ablation or negative steering reduces it.
5. Novel-case generalization
The effect appears on new contradictions that were not present in the training or discovery dataset.
6. Cross-model recurrence
Functionally similar structures appear in independently trained model families.
7. Controlled emergence
A model trained without explicit TRIZ materials develops comparable internal operators from recurring unlabeled problem–solution structures.

The seventh result would be the strongest.

It would be difficult to explain it as direct retrieval of TRIZ documentation.


What Would Falsify or Weaken the Hypothesis?
The project must be designed to fail clearly.

Evidence against the hypothesis would include:

apparent principle clusters disappearing when vocabulary is controlled;
probes working within one domain but failing on held-out domains;
steering increasing use of principle-related words without changing the actual solution strategy;
all principle vectors producing the same generic increase in creativity;
interventions damaging fluency rather than redirecting reasoning;
representations appearing only after explicit TRIZ prompting;
effects existing only in instruction-tuned models and not in base models;
no reliable ablation effect;
failure to replicate across newly written problems or model families;
failure of controlled models to develop any shared transformation structure.

It is also possible that only a subset of TRIZ will emerge.

Some principles may represent genuinely general cognitive operators, while others may be historically useful engineering heuristics tied to particular technologies and physical effects.

That would itself be a major finding.


What Would a Positive Result Mean?
A positive result would not prove that an LLM is conscious.

It would not demonstrate subjective experience.

It would not prove that its understanding is identical to human understanding.

It would not settle every philosophical question about meaning, embodiment, or grounding.

It would also not invalidate the broader concerns raised in the original Stochastic Parrots paper regarding training data, bias, environmental costs, scale, documentation, and social consequences. citeturn442735search0

The more precise target is the popularized, strongest version of the stochastic-parrot position:

An LLM does nothing more than recombine surface linguistic patterns and contains no reusable abstract representations that causally participate in its behavior.

If an unlabeled, domain-general inventive operator could be decoded, transferred across domains, injected, removed, composed with other operators, and shown to redirect novel problem solving, that strong surface-only explanation would become extremely difficult to defend.

The decisive distinction is this:

An LLM is unquestionably stochastic. The question is whether stochastic optimization produces structured internal models.

Stochasticity and internal structure are not opposites.

Othello-GPT already provides a controlled example in which next-token prediction produced a causally relevant representation of an underlying game state. Project Latent TRIZ would ask whether something analogous occurs for abstract transformations of problems, rather than only for a clearly defined board. citeturn654930academia26

A successful experiment would therefore provide evidence that the model has learned part of the structure behind human inventive behavior:

not only which words follow other words;
not only which solution resembles a previous solution;
but which abstract transformation can convert one constrained problem state into a more desirable one.

That is a limited but meaningful form of modeling the world and the intelligence expressed within it.


What This Could Teach Us About TRIZ
The experiment is not only about validating LLMs.

It could also turn LLM interpretability into a new instrument for studying TRIZ itself.

We might discover that:

some principles form very stable cross-domain representations;
some principles collapse into a shared latent operator;
some are combinations of more primitive transformations;
some are context-dependent rather than universal;
some are difficult to distinguish computationally;
models contain useful inventive operators that are not cleanly represented by the traditional 40 principles.

This raises a provocative possibility:

TRIZ may be a human-readable projection of a larger latent algebra of inventive transformations.

Altshuller and his collaborators identified regularities by examining the products of human invention.

Language models may discover related regularities by compressing a vast portion of recorded human thought.

The two processes are different, but both may be converging on structures imposed by the problem itself:

systems have components;
resources are limited;
properties conflict;
actions have side effects;
constraints can be separated in space, time, scale, or condition;
useful transformations recur across domains.

Perhaps these are not merely conventions of TRIZ.

Perhaps they are natural coordinates of inventive problem solving.


Why This Matters for Matryca
Matryca Plumber began from the problem of memory: how humans and agents can safely maintain the same durable body of knowledge without surrendering ownership to an opaque database or cloud provider.

Matryca Brain extends the question from storing knowledge to learning which knowledge matters and how it should evolve. citeturn686708search0

But memory alone is not intelligence.

A cognitive system also needs reusable ways to transform what it knows:

identify contradictions;
reframe problems;
move between levels of abstraction;
generate alternatives;
transfer solutions across domains;
learn which transformations work.

TRIZ provides an explicit human framework for these operations.

Mechanistic interpretability may reveal whether related operations already exist inside language models.

In that sense:

Matryca Plumber investigates the architecture of shared memory.
Matryca Brain investigates adaptive knowledge.
Project Latent TRIZ investigates the operators of inventive cognition.


An Invitation to Build This Experiment in Public
I am proposing Project Latent TRIZ as an open research initiative.

It needs several kinds of expertise:
TRIZ practitioners
To select principles, classify examples, identify ambiguous cases, create valid contradictions, and prevent superficial interpretations of the methodology.
Mechanistic interpretability researchers
To design probes, activation interventions, sparse feature analyses, Jacobian-lens experiments, causal controls, and cross-model comparisons.
Open-model developers
To make the experiments reproducible on Gemma, Qwen, and other inspectable model families.
Patent and innovation specialists
To contribute historically and technically sound problem–solution examples.
Cognitive scientists and philosophers of AI
To help distinguish what the results demonstrate from what we may be tempted to project onto them.

My intention is to make the protocol, dataset, code, evaluation criteria, results, and failures publicly inspectable.

The first discussion should not begin with the assumption that the hypothesis is true.

It should begin with the harder question:

What experiment would genuinely distinguish an abstract, causally active inventive operator from memorization, lexical similarity, prompting effects, or evaluator projection?

That is the standard this project must meet.


Conclusion
Altshuller asked whether invention contains repeatable patterns.

Modern interpretability research asks whether apparently intelligent behavior is supported by inspectable internal representations.

Project Latent TRIZ brings those two questions onto the same experimental bench.

Perhaps the 40 Inventive Principles are only a useful historical methodology.

Perhaps language models merely reproduce TRIZ patterns they encountered during training.

Or perhaps Altshuller and his collaborators extracted a visible fragment of something deeper: a compact algebra of transformations toward which both human analysis and machine learning converge when exposed to enough examples of invention.

That is not yet a conclusion.

It is now a falsifiable hypothesis.

And it is worth testing in public.
